# The 13 thumbnail archetypes

Distilled from `types-of-thumbnail.pdf` (Internet Stamp), which claims these
13 formats cover ~91% of YouTube. Ten are **premises** — what the picture
claims. Three are **treatments** — how densely you render it. Pick one of
each; they compose.

## 1. Duck out of Water

**Mechanism.** Put the subject somewhere they visibly do not belong. The
mismatch itself generates a stack of questions — how did they get there,
how will everyone react, what happens next — and none are answerable from
the image.

**Reach for it when** you have a person, animal, or object that is wrong
for its setting, or can be made wrong for one.

**Examples.** A duck standing outside a Gucci store ("I took my duck to
Gucci", 3.3M). An untanned, unmuscled guy on stage between two oiled
bodybuilders ("I Entered Mr Universe - With No Training"). Goofy fronting a
death metal band. A grandpa on a Fashion Week runway, captioned FAKE MODEL.

**Failure mode.** The mismatch is too mild to read in a glance, or it needs
a caption to explain that a mismatch exists. If you have to label the duck,
find a bigger duck.

## 2. Puzzle to be Solved

**Mechanism.** State a fact that doesn't add up. Humans are wired to close
the loop — the source's line is that puzzles are practice for problem
solving — and the only way to close this one is the video.

**Reach for it when** your content contains a counterintuitive statistic,
ratio, or observation. Also the go-to rescue for a boring photo: the right
overlaid question converts an ordinary scene into a puzzle.

**Examples.** A US map split 20%/80% by population against an even split of
land. A man aiming a fan at a window with "Further away is better?" (8.2M —
without the text, the same photo is nothing). Trees labelled "No-Touching
Zone". "NYC's tallest buildings are HALF EMPTY". Two photos of the same
fish species 50 years apart.

**Failure mode.** The puzzle is trivially solvable from the thumbnail, or
the viewer doesn't care about the domain enough to want it solved.

## 3. Apples to Oranges

**Mechanism.** Compare two things that shouldn't be compared. Like-for-like
is a school exercise; scale and category mismatch is the interesting one,
because you only learn how extreme something is by putting it next to its
opposite.

**Reach for it when** you have two subjects separated by a huge gap in
size, price, era, or category.

**Examples.** World's smallest truck beside world's largest truck. A gorilla
next to a strongman. A new Prius and a 30-year-old Bentley, both labelled
$35,000. A $649 Dyson fan versus a $15 Walmart fan. A tier list ranking
every animal against every other animal.

**Failure mode.** Comparing two similar things. If both items belong to the
same bracket, there is no tension and no reason to look.

## 4. Level Up

**Mechanism.** Show the start and the end. If the distance between them is
big enough, the viewer needs to know the route — and quietly concludes that
if it worked for that person, it might work for them.

**Reach for it when** you have a genuine transformation: a build, a body, a
skill, a restoration, a career. The "before" earns recognition ("this
person knows where I'm coming from"); the "after" earns authority.

**Examples.** WEEK 1 / WEEK 12 physiques. Gordon Ramsay as a boy and as
himself. BEFORE / AFTER eggs in a stainless pan. Dirt in a hand → a
polished sphere labelled $100. BROKE → $100M. A can of tuna → a plated
meal. 9:00 PM / 9:01 PM clothing hack.

**Failure mode.** The gap is too small to read at thumbnail size, or the
before is as appealing as the after. Also: if the method is obvious from
the two images, there's nothing to click for.

## 5. War of 2 Worlds

**Mechanism.** Two forces set against each other, mid-collision or about to
be. Conflict is a story engine: A versus B with the outcome unresolved.
The conflict can be sought (a challenge) or found (something that happened
to the creator), and it can be internal.

**Reach for it when** you have opposing sides — groups, a person against
nature, a person against themselves.

**Examples.** 100 kids vs 100 adults; 100 boys vs 100 girls (MrBeast, ~250M
each). A cyclist crashed into a police car parked in a bike lane (Casey
Neistat, 30M — soft, low-res, still works). A guy breaking silly laws in
front of a police officer. Paddling a block of ice across the world's
hottest lake — man vs nature. Sitting underwater holding a weight down —
man vs himself.

**Failure mode.** The sides aren't legible as sides, or the conflict is
already resolved in the frame. Keep it at the moment before the outcome.

## 6. Talkback

**Mechanism.** *You say this — I say that.* Position the video as a reply:
to another creator, to a whole subject, to received wisdom. Disagreement is
inherently suspenseful because the viewer wants to see who's right.

**Reach for it when** you're rebutting, reacting, correcting, or arguing a
contrarian position.

**Examples.** "what they missed" over a laptop showing the original video's
thumbnail. "the problem with INTERIOR DESIGN…". "SHORT KING" over a
fighter, arguing short is better in a sport that worships reach. Snoop Dogg
reacting to a remix of his own track.

**Failure mode.** The viewer doesn't know or care about the thing being
rebutted. A talkback needs a target the audience already recognizes — show
the target in the frame if it isn't famous.

## 7. Text

**Treatment.** *When you can't show it, tell it.*

**Mechanism.** Some ideas have no photograph: entropy, rejection, sensory
deprivation, abstractions of every kind. Writing is the oldest fallback,
and it stands out precisely because most of a scroll is imagery.

**Reach for it when** the concept resists being pictured, or when the right
few words transform an otherwise ordinary image. Note the source's caveat:
the general advice is *use pictures, not words* — text is what you reach
for when the picture genuinely can't carry it, not a default.

**Examples.** "How MIT Decides Who to Reject in 30 Seconds" on a plain dark
card. "ENTROPY" over abstract colour (17M). "no light / no sound / no
gravity" for a sensory deprivation tank. "Looks stupid. Actually genius."
over a zigzag search pattern — the *I-know-what-you're-thinking* variant,
where the text names the viewer's own reaction and then contradicts it.
"HOW TO SKIN A WATERMELON" — the word choice is the whole thumbnail.

**Failure mode.** Text that merely labels the topic. "CAMERA ANGLES 101" on
a black card is skippable; the same video with a grid of famous shots
behind it is not. Label = boring, question or claim = click.

## 8. Minimalism

**Treatment.** One thing, and nothing else.

**Mechanism.** In a scroll of clutter, emptiness is the pattern break. All
the video's energy focuses into a single point — the source's image is a
lighthouse.

**Reach for it when** the single subject is genuinely arresting on its own,
or when it is instantly meaningful to a specific audience even if outsiders
won't read it. Requires real confidence: you are going all-in on one
element, and the source is explicit that it *looks* easy and is actually
hard.

**Examples.** A plain white plastic chair on black, "How This Chair
Conquered the World" (3.2M). Vsauce's "Where Is This Video?" with no
thumbnail at all. A creepy yellow bear filling the frame. Panda-patterned
handmade candy — meaningless to most people, irresistible to the niche that
knows what it is.

**Failure mode.** The one thing isn't interesting. There is no second
element to rescue it, which is the bet you took.

---

## 9. Maximalism

**Treatment.** Many elements, one story.

**Mechanism.** Density that reads as richness rather than noise. Every
element is a separate hook, and the viewer can enter through whichever one
catches them — choose your own adventure. The promise is that the video is
as thorough as the image is full.

**Reach for it when** the subject genuinely is complicated — geopolitics, a
complete guide, an exhaustive taste test, deliberate chaos — and you can
make every element serve the same story.

**Examples.** Johnny Harris's Bosnia thumbnail: his own face (brand), a
tank (war), refugees walking (the human cost), the word BOSNIA (subject),
Bill Clinton (politics), a map behind it all (geography) — any one alone
would be nothing. Drew Gooden surrounded by streaming logos, strike
placards and review scores. Erik The Electric buried in fast food. James
Hoffmann behind thirty coffee creamers. A camera-angles guide tiled with
famous shots.

**Failure mode.** Clutter. The source's own warning: *use at your own risk,
because you could just end up with a big mess.* If the elements don't
cohere, and if there's no focal point inside the density, it fails.

## 10. I Did Something Crazy

**Mechanism.** *How is that possible? Did they actually do this?* An act so
extreme or absurd that the viewer has to verify it. The promise is high and
delivery must be higher.

**Reach for it when** the feat genuinely is implausible, and the image
proves it happened rather than just claiming it.

**Examples.** Flying to a McDonald's drive-thru on a paramotor. DEEP FRIED
COFFEE. "I Counted To 100,000!" — where the *timestamp* of 23:48:07 is the
crazy part. Garlic bread, an egg, a pizza slice, and eventually people,
sent to the edge of space.

**Failure mode.** Shelf life. The source is emphatic: crazy decays into
normal, and normal is close to boring. Space stunts were extraordinary once
and then everyone did them. Check what your niche has already normalized.

---

## 11. Cautionary Tale

**Mechanism.** Show the viewer a pain they might be walking into. Loss
aversion is stronger than desire, so the pull to avoid a mistake beats the
pull toward a gain — *they show me pain, I say I'm scared of that pain, and
I click to figure out how to avoid it.*

**Reach for it when** there's a common mistake in your subject. Works on
two audiences at once: beginners who need the warning, and experts who
click for the smug confirmation that they already knew.

**Examples.** WRONG stamped on a PB&J. "LUCID DREAMING RISKS" with a shark.
A red circle-slash over cheap lumber, "NEVER use this to make furniture".
WRONG! / STOP over a pull-up. "7.5 YEARS WASTED…". "STOP USING THIS" over
Tyvek house wrap.

**Failure mode.** Giving away the fix. If the thumbnail says which lumber
and why, there is no reason to watch. Name the pain, withhold the remedy.

---

## 12. I Want That

**Mechanism.** The inverse of the cautionary tale — not *how do I avoid
that pain* but *how do I get that thing I love.* Show the desirable end
state; the video is the recipe.

**Reach for it when** there's a covetable object or outcome. Attainability
raises it: a 99-cent notebook anyone can buy beats an unreachable luxury.
Framing it as a secret raises it further — the source's line is that the
only thing people love more than solutions is secret solutions.

**Examples.** A perfectly organized 3D-printed drawer insert. Shoulders in
a dumbbell workout thumbnail. "$0.99 SECRET" pointing at a pocket notebook
(4M) — the price and the item are given away; the *method* is the secret
being sold.

**Failure mode.** Showing the thing without implying a route to it. Desire
plus no recipe is an ad, not a thumbnail.

---

## 13. Cliffhanger

**Mechanism.** Freeze the moment before the outcome. Cut straight to the
interesting part with no setup, so the viewer needs both halves — how did
this happen, and what happens next. TV opens episodes this way for the same
reason.

**Reach for it when** you have a frozen instant of unresolved action, or
can stage one. There's also a pure-text variant: a sentence cut off before
its payload.

**Examples.** A man strapped down surrounded by rats, "If You Scream, You
Lose". Hands dragging someone under. A leopard walking toward a person on a
road. A Jeep tipping over an actual cliff edge. A parrot leaning toward an
oblivious cat. A wolf in a field, "What to expect if you encounter a wolf"
(21M). "THIS is why your dog doesn't come" — text cliffhanger, the *this*
withheld. The source's counterexample: turkeys standing in grass, nothing
about to happen, definitely not a cliffhanger.

**Failure mode.** The moment is already resolved, or nothing appears to be
in motion. If the frame is stable, there's nothing to wonder about.
